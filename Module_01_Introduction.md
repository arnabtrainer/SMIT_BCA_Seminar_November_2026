# Module 1 — Introduction: Discover the Power of AI

## AI with Applications — Learn, Create & Build Your Future

**Venue:** Saroj Mohan Institute of Technology (SMIT), Guptipara  
**Audience:** Prospective BCA students, newly admitted students, and participating senior BCA/AI–ML students  
**Duration:** 15 minutes; a 10-minute fallback is provided  
**Format:** Five short slides, audience participation, and one brief live demonstration  
**Prerequisites:** No programming or AI knowledge; students do not need a device or account for this module  
**Document:** Complete trainer guide, version 1.0 — 20 October 2026

> **Core message:** AI can help us learn, create and build—but we must still think, check and practise.

**How to use this guide:** Put only the **On the screen** content on slides. Use **Trainer's speaking notes** as a guide, not a script that must be read word for word. Preparation, reference material and fallback instructions are outside the 15-minute delivery time. Start the module clock after the college's formal introduction; do not repeat your professional profile.

---

## 1. 🎯 Learning objectives

By the end of this module, students should be able to:

- Recognise familiar AI-supported activities and understand that not every computer operation is AI.
- Describe AI, Machine Learning and Generative AI in simple language.
- Give an AI assistant a clear request with context and a desired output.
- Explain why checking answers, protecting personal information and practising independently still matter.

**Success check:** Students can name one everyday AI application, describe one upcoming project, and state one precaution. This is an informal check—not the seminar's final quiz.

**Have you already used AI today—perhaps without noticing?**

**No coding knowledge required. Bring your curiosity.**

## 4. 🌍 Slide 2 — AI Is Already Around Us

**Time:** 02:00–05:00

### On the screen

| Familiar experience | The useful task |
|---|---|
| Video recommendations | Suggest something you may want to watch |
| Traffic and journey estimates | Help predict how long a trip may take |
| AI-powered translation | Help work across languages |

**AI is a useful part of many applications—not a guarantee of a correct result.**

*Trainer references: YouTube recommendations [3], Google Maps traffic prediction [4], and Google's introduction to machine learning [2].*

### Trainer's speaking notes

> “When a video platform recommends something, it is trying to find content that may interest you. Google Maps uses machine learning as part of its traffic prediction. Translation tools can help us work with another language. These are different tasks, not one machine doing everything.
>
> Notice the benefit: finding something relevant, planning a journey, or understanding information. Also notice the limitation: a recommendation may be unsuitable, a journey may take longer, and a translation may need correction.”

Revisit the opening poll briefly: “Would anyone now change their answer?” No second round of introductions is necessary.

### Important distinction

> “A basic calculator follows fixed arithmetic instructions. It does not need to learn from examples to multiply two numbers. So, being digital or automatic does not, by itself, make something AI.”

**Trainer note:** Discuss the specific feature, not the whole product. A navigation or video app also contains many ordinary software components.

### Visual suggestion

Use three large icons: a play button, a route and a translation symbol. No live app switching is needed.

**Transition:** “Let us understand three names you will keep hearing: AI, Machine Learning and Generative AI.”

## 5. 🧠 Slide 3 — AI, ML and Generative AI—Without the Jargon

**Time:** 05:00–09:00

### On the screen

| Term | Beginner-friendly meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Artificial Intelligence (AI)** | The broad field of making computers perform tasks associated with intelligence | Recognising patterns or recommending an action |
| **Machine Learning (ML)** | An approach within AI that learns patterns from data | Learning from examples to identify likely spam |
| **Generative AI** | AI that produces content in response to input | Creating an explanation, image, video or code |

**AI is the umbrella. ML is one approach within it. Modern generative AI commonly uses ML.** [1][2]

### Trainer's speaking notes

Use one connected example rather than three unrelated technical stories:

> “Suppose we want to identify unwanted emails. We could write a fixed rule, such as ‘flag an email containing a certain phrase.’ That rule is something a person has specified.
>
> With a machine-learning approach, we can train a model using examples of unwanted and ordinary emails. The model learns patterns that help it classify new messages. It can still make mistakes.
>
> Now consider a different task: asking an assistant to write a new explanation or generate a short video. That is generative AI—the system is producing content rather than just assigning a category.” [2]

Explain the relationship in one sentence:

> “Machine Learning belongs within the wider field of AI; Generative AI describes content-creating capabilities. These names are related, not three competing products.” [1][2]

### Reinforce only these distinctions

**Recognising or predicting is not the same task as generating.** A system that identifies likely spam need not create any new content.

**A fluent answer is not proof of correctness.** Generated information may be mistaken even when the wording sounds confident. [6]

### Visual suggestion

Show a large **AI** umbrella with **Learning from data** beneath it. Add two example cards: **Recognise / Predict** and **Generate**. Explain that this is a simplified teaching illustration, not a complete classification of AI.

**Do not introduce here:** neural-network layers, model architectures, mathematics, training algorithms, model rankings or the history of AI. Do not imply that every chatbot conversation immediately retrains its underlying model.

**Transition:** “You do not need to memorise these terms today. Let us connect them with things you can actually do.”

## 6. 🚀 Slide 4 — What Can AI Help Me Do?

**Time:** 09:00–12:00

### On the screen

# Learn → Create → Build

| Your goal | Our seminar project | Your responsibility |
|---|---|---|
| **Learn** | Turn introductory study notes into learning resources | Check the source and practise |
| **Create** | Develop a short “My BCA, My Future” video | Direct the story and review the result |
| **Build** | Create a simple Smart Study Planner | Test whether it works |

**AI assistance does not replace your understanding.**

### Trainer's speaking notes

> “First, we will explore a learning companion. Instead of only reading notes, we will ask questions about them and explore other ways of revising.
>
> Next, we will become creators. We will see how a written idea can become a short video. We will still have to choose the message and check what the AI produces.
>
> Finally, time permitting, we will build a small study planner. Otherwise, we will explore the prepared working version. You will see the result either way.”

The notebook project can use uploaded sources for answers and produce learning resources such as study guides and Audio Overviews. Checking a citation against the original source remains part of the demonstration. [8][9]

### Why this matters to an aspiring BCA student

> “I encourage you to move from only using applications to asking how they work and how you could improve them. Practise problem-solving, programming, working with data and explaining your ideas. When AI helps generate code, your understanding helps you judge whether it is useful.”

This is learning guidance, **not a claim about SMIT's exact syllabus, admission requirements or placement outcomes**.

### Audience interaction 2 — Choose your interest

Ask: **“Which interests you most today: learning from your notes, making a video, or building an application?”**

Invite a show of hands for each. Acknowledge the responses and continue; do not turn this into an additional discussion segment.

### Trainer notes

The planner will be **AI-assisted in its creation**. The completed app may simply run ordinary, fixed software logic; do not suggest that an AI model must be operating inside it.

Use the project name **NotebookLM learning companion** consistently when speaking. Google's current help pages use **Gemini Notebook**; show the product name visible in your rehearsed account once, without spending teaching time on branding. [8][9]

### Visual suggestion

Three cards—an open book, a film frame and a simple app window—with one short caption each. Avoid introducing more tools here.

**Transition:** “Before our first project, let us try one tiny example of asking an AI assistant clearly—and checking its answer.”

## 7. 💬 Slide 5 — Ask Clearly. Check Carefully.

**Time:** 12:00–15:00, including the transition to Module 2

### On the screen

**A useful prompt = Context + Task + Desired output**

**Vague:** “Explain algorithms.”

**Clearer:** “Explain an algorithm to a beginner using four steps for packing a college bag.”

**Check the answer • Protect private information • Practise yourself**

*Clear instructions and relevant context can improve usefulness; they cannot guarantee factual accuracy. [5][6]*

### The three-minute sequence

| Time within this slide | Trainer action |
|---|---|
| First 20 seconds | Contrast the vague and clearer request; explain “prompt” as the instruction given to the tool |
| Next 60 seconds | Submit the prepared prompt and highlight the resulting example |
| Next 45 seconds | Ask the audience to check whether the example makes sense |
| Next 30 seconds | Give the three precautions below |
| Final 25 seconds | Introduce the NotebookLM learning project |

### One-minute live demonstration

**Tool:** Your already-tested ChatGPT account in a fresh chat. Use text chat only; do not demonstrate model selection, account setup, uploads or other features.

**Purpose:** Show how an unfamiliar word can become a familiar example—and let students evaluate it. This is not a separate lesson on writing emails or generic text generation.

**Copy-ready prompt:**

```text
I am considering a BCA course and have not studied programming.

Explain the word “algorithm” using the everyday example of packing
my college bag.

Give:
1. Four short, numbered steps in a sensible order.
2. A one-sentence definition in simple English.
3. One question asking what could go wrong if I skip an important step.

Keep the complete response under 90 words. Do not include code.
```

Paste the prompt; do not type it slowly on stage. Run only the clearer prompt. The vague version is a comparison on the slide, not a second live generation. [5]

### Illustrative output—not an actual recorded tool response

> 1. Check tomorrow's timetable.
> 2. List the books and materials you need.
> 3. Put those items into your bag.
> 4. Check the list once more before closing the bag.
>
> An algorithm is a clear sequence of steps for completing a task.
>
> What could go wrong if you skip checking tomorrow's timetable?

This is a trainer-written everyday analogy. A formal computing algorithm requires sufficiently precise steps; the example is not a complete computer program. The basic definition is supported by NIST's algorithm reference. [7]

### Audience interaction 3 — Check, do not just admire

Ask: **“Does the order make sense? What could go wrong if the timetable check is skipped?”**

**Expected response:** “We may pack the wrong books or miss something important.” Accept sensible alternatives.

Point out: “You have just checked an AI answer using your own understanding. That is the habit we want.”

### If the live result is weak or delayed

If nothing useful appears after approximately 20 seconds, use the saved, rehearsed output. Say plainly, **“This is a response I prepared earlier with the same prompt.”** Do not claim that the saved example was generated live.

If the response contains an error, identify it and ask how it could be corrected. Do not spend the remaining module time repeatedly regenerating. If no rehearsed output is available, use the trainer-written illustration above and label it as such.

### Three precautions—approximately 30 seconds

> **“Check it:** compare important answers with reliable sources. AI can sound confident and still be wrong. [6]
>
> **Protect it:** do not put passwords, identity documents or other people's private information into a classroom prompt. Obtain permission before using identifiable photos or voices.
>
> **Practise it:** use the explanation to learn. Do not submit an answer you cannot explain, and follow your institution's rules.”

### Transition to Module 2

> “We have asked AI to explain a familiar example. What happens when we give it our own study notes? Could those notes become questions, a mind map or an audio discussion—and could we check where an answer came from?
>
> Let us explore our first project: **AI Can Help Me Learn**.” [8][9]

**Trainer action:** Move to the prepared notebook tab. The actual upload and notebook walkthrough belong to Module 2.

## 10. 📝 Trainer-only reference notes

### Possible applications beyond student life

These are optional discussion examples, **not seven additional teaching topics**. Mention at most one if a student asks. They illustrate tasks AI may support, not guaranteed outcomes.

| Area | Example problem or task |
|---|---|
| Education | Explain a passage and generate practice questions from learning material [8] |
| Healthcare | Support trained professionals in interpreting patterns in information [1] |
| Banking | Flag unusual transaction patterns for investigation [1] |
| Agriculture | Explore whether crop photographs or weather data can help identify problems; present this as a possible project, not a diagnosis |
| Transportation | Estimate travel time using traffic information [4] |
| Entertainment | Recommend relevant videos [3] |
| Software development | Draft code that a person must inspect and test [1] |

### Likely questions and brief suggested responses

**“Do I need programming knowledge to participate today?”**  
“No. You can participate by asking questions and checking results. Programming becomes useful when you want to understand, customise and maintain an application.”

**“Will AI make my learning unnecessary?”**  
“Do not treat it that way. You still need enough understanding to spot errors, explain your decisions and solve a problem when the tool is unavailable.”

**“Can I believe an answer because it sounds confident?”**  
“No. Check important information against a reliable source. Fluency is not evidence of accuracy.” [6]

- [ ] Module 2 is ready; the formal quiz and feedback remain in Module 5.

**Closing takeaway:** “Be curious enough to try AI—and thoughtful enough to check it.”
