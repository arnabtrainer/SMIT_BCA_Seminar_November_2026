# 🔐 PRIVATE TRAINER GUIDE — Module 1: Discover the Power of AI

**Programme:** AI in Action: Learn • Create • Build  
**Status:** Internal preparation and presentation notes — do not distribute  
**Core session:** Approximately **15 minutes**, adjustable for the audience  
**Audience adaptation:** Prospective students, undergraduate/postgraduate learners, faculty, and working professionals

> Never project or distribute this document. The audience-facing Markdown and public PowerPoint are duration-neutral and contain no operational instructions.

## 🎯 Trainer's Objective

Use Module 1 to open warmly, establish the difference between everyday AI and generative AI, set expectations for **Learn → Create → Build**, and teach the essential skill of checking AI output. Avoid lengthy product tours or technical depth here.

## 🎤 Speaker Notes and Interaction by Slide

### Slide 1 — Discover the Power of AI

**Suggested introduction:** “Artificial intelligence is not just about sophisticated robots or coding. In this session, we will experience how AI can help us understand information, create something original, and build useful solutions. No prior programming knowledge is needed to follow today's introduction.”

**Visual:** Abstract neural-network theme. **Avoid:** repeating the host's speaker profile.

### Slide 2 — Have you used AI today?

**Say:** “How many of you have already used AI today—perhaps without realising it?” Invite a show of hands. If students mention chatbots, acknowledge them and move to recommendations, maps, and translation. Respond warmly to “not sure.”

**If quiet:** “Did any of you watch a recommended video or check a traffic estimate today?”

### Slide 3 — AI is already around us

**Say:** “Recommendation systems can help suggest relevant videos. Navigation systems use prediction to estimate traffic and travel times. Translation uses language-processing technologies. None of these features is perfect.”

**Precision:** Apps mix ordinary software with AI features. Avoid saying every recommendation always comes from the same algorithm or claiming that predictions cannot fail.

### Slide 4 — Not every automatic task is AI

**Say:** “A calculator follows predefined arithmetic operations. A machine-learning spam classifier can learn statistical patterns from examples and then use those patterns to classify new messages. Automation is a broader idea; not all automation uses AI.”

**Optional extension:** Ask “Does an alarm clock need to understand what it is doing?” Explain simple rules versus pattern-learning, without turning it into a technical debate.

### Slide 5 — AI, ML and generative AI

**Say:** “Artificial Intelligence is the broad field. Machine Learning is an approach within it that learns from data. Generative AI is best known for producing new content, including text, images, video, audio and code. Most modern generative systems use ML, but not all AI systems are generative.”

**Avoid:** Model architecture, deep learning derivations, false claim that every prompt directly retrains the model.

### Slide 6 — Learn • Create • Build

**Say:** “Our next experiences have one simple progression. First, learn from documents. Then create a short video. Finally, see how an idea can become a practical planner app. You do not need to master all the tools today—notice the problem we are solving.”

**Audience polling:** Ask which area is most interesting. Limit to a fast show of hands.

**Important:** Project 4 is optional for full live construction in shorter events; the prebuilt planner remains available.

### Slide 7 — Ask clearly

**Say:** “A prompt is a request to the AI. Give it context, say what task you want, and tell it what a useful answer should look like. We are not trying to learn secret magic words; we are learning how to communicate our goal.”

**Prompt comparison:** `Explain algorithms.` vs the clearer day-trip version. Good prompts help but are not guarantees.

### Slide 8 — Live demo

**Preparation:** Open a signed-in AI assistant in advance; keep a clean chat window and copied prompt. In the default plan do just one generation.

**Exact prompt:**

```text
Explain the idea of an algorithm to someone with no programming background.

Use a familiar example: planning a one-day trip.

Give:
1. Four sensible steps in the right order.
2. A one-sentence definition of an algorithm.
3. One question about what could go wrong if an important step is skipped.

Use simple English. Do not include code. Keep the reply under 100 words.
```

**Interpretation:** Point out the sequence, the plain-language definition, and the self-check question. Don't claim a successful generated answer is necessarily accurate.

### Slide 9 — Read, review and improve

**Say:** “This plan sounds tidy, but would you leave immediately after these four steps? What would you still check?” Expect possible answers: route, travel time, budget, opening hours, accessibility, safety, weather.

**If live demo fails:** Use the static illustrative four-step plan already on this slide. It is intentionally simple and does not present made-up travel times as facts.

### Slide 10 — Responsible AI

**Say:** “Check important answers, protect private information, and practise skills yourself. AI is useful when we stay responsible for what we accept and publish.”

**Corporate adaptation:** Replace examples of student records with customer data, internal documents, and company policies in verbal explanations, not on public slides.

### Slide 11 — Transition to learning companion

**Transition:** “We have seen AI explain something in a friendly way. Now imagine asking questions about your own notes or reports—then turning those materials into a quiz, a mind map, and even an audio discussion. That is where we go next.”

**Product note:** Current Google documentation refers to Gemini Notebook, formerly NotebookLM. Use product branding shown by the tested current interface; do not spend introductory time explaining brand changes.
